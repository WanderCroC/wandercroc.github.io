Job Market Paper

Differentially Private Market Segmentation

Abstract

We study how differential privacy constrains price discrimination and the attainable combinations of consumer and producer surplus. Every segmentation that maximizes consumer surplus leaves the seller earning its uniform monopoly profit. Privacy can nevertheless reduce the gains available to consumers by restricting the information needed to support discounts. Sufficiently strict privacy permits only uniform monopoly pricing. These results show why protecting consumer data need not improve consumer welfare through its effects on prices and trade.

Strategic Information Disclosure in Algorithmic Pricing

With Zexin Ye

Abstract

We study how information disclosure shapes the behavior of Q-learning pricing algorithms under uncertain demand. Selectively revealing low-demand states while pooling high-demand states yields higher profits than full disclosure. We also find a reversal of the theoretical benchmark: no disclosure produces higher profits when algorithms are sufficiently patient, while full disclosure performs better when they are less patient. The reversal persists under both linear and logit demand. Restricting information sharing can therefore raise profits in markets with algorithmic pricing.

Communicating with Self-Confidence

Abstract

How do people address self-confidence-concerned audiences? This paper analyzes this question under the framework of strategic communication games proposed by Crawford and Sobel (1982) by assuming the receiver cares about the expectation of his ability, i.e., his self-confidence. No fully revealing equilibrium exists even if the sender’s and the receiver’s preferences are aligned. Indeed, all equilibria in this game are interval partitional as in Crawford and Sobel (1982). In the uniform quadratic environment, the partition equilibria resemble those in standard cheap talk games.

Endogenous Cheap Talk in Algorithmic Pricing

With Zexin Ye

Abstract

We study how pricing algorithms learn to communicate when firms hold private information about demand. Our theoretical benchmark compares profit-maximizing pooling and separating communication in repeated price competition. In the numerical baseline, greater patience can shift communication from pooling to separation; with highly precise signals, separation can give way to pooling before returning. We are developing Q-learning experiments in which algorithms choose both messages and prices to test whether learned communication follows these predictions.
